Radish is the one green we grow that sorts people. Nobody tries it and shrugs.

Bite one and you get the peppery snap of a whole radish, clean and sharp and gone in a second. It is brighter than mustard and a good deal sharper than [arugula](/blog/arugula-before-it-gets-bitter/), which is saying something, because arugula is not a shy green either. We grow a daikon variety, because the heat comes through cleanest that way, without the muddy bitterness that some radish seed brings along with it. It is the most opinionated tray on our shelves. People tend to land hard on one side or the other within a bite, and we have stopped trying to talk anybody across the line.

We do not treat that as a problem. When we hand somebody [pea tendrils](/blog/pea-tendrils-the-gateway-green/), we are trying to get a yes out of a whole table. Radish is for the person who already knows what they want out of a green.

## The fastest thing in the shed

Radish is also our quickest crop, six to eight days from seed to cut. Nothing else we grow turns around that fast. It comes barreling out of [the blackout stack](/blog/the-blackout-phase/) while the slower seed is still deciding, and it greens up under the lights almost the same day.

We cut radish at the cotyledon stage, while the crop is still those first two rounded seed leaves, before any true leaves show. That is on purpose. Let radish run past that point and the flavor turns coarse and the stems get stringy. Cut it at the right hour and you get the whole pepper hit with none of the toughness.

Which is worth a footnote on [the home growing post](/blog/can-i-grow-microgreens-at-home/). We told anybody starting a first tray to buy pea seed, then kale, and a few people have reasonably asked why we skipped the fast one. Speed is exactly the reason. A pea tray will wait for you. It looks about the same on day ten as it did on day nine, and if the weekend gets away from you the crop is forgiving about it. Radish gives you a window closer to a day, and it does not care what else is on your calendar. It is a great crop to grow once you can read a tray. It is a rough one to learn on.

## Where the heat belongs

Radish is not a salad you sit down to by the bowl. It is a tool, and it does the same job an acid does. Anywhere a dish is rich enough that you want a squeeze of lime or a pickled onion, radish will get you there instead, and it brings crunch that neither of those has.

Fatty fish is the clearest case. A pile on smoked salmon and cream cheese is close to perfect. Same on a banh mi, where it sits in with the pickled vegetables like it was always in the recipe. It belongs on avocado toast, which is the pairing everybody arrives at on their own eventually. And it goes on a bowl of ramen or pho at the last possible second, after the bowl is off the stove, so it wilts just slightly and keeps its bite.

Tacos are the other obvious one, and that turned into [a whole post of its own](/blog/taco-night-needs-a-crunch/) back in July, including how much to use and why the bowl goes on the table instead of in the pan.

Nutritionally it carries a real load. Radish micros are high in vitamin C, vitamin B6, and folate, and they bring sulforaphane, the same compound that gets broccoli micros most of their attention. Research suggests that family of compounds is worth eating. We will leave it there, because the honest reason to put radish on your plate is that it makes the plate better.

If you have been ordering the mild end of our list and you are curious what the other end tastes like, [add radish to your next box](/greens/radish/). Start with a small pile on something rich. You will know within one bite which side of the line you are on.
