This spotlight was scheduled for the thirteenth. We moved it up a week for the plainest possible reason: chard is on the rack right now and it is the best looking tray in the shed. Radish, which was first in line for today, is still out of rotation, and we are not going to write a spotlight for something you cannot order.

So, chard.

## What you are looking at

We grow a Rainbow variety, which means the stems come up in hot pink, yellow, orange, and a deep red that reads almost purple against the cream of the tray. Not one color per tray either. They come up mixed, so a single cutting looks like a handful of colored glass rods with leaves on top.

The pigments doing that are betalains, the same family of compounds that make a beet a beet, which is not a coincidence. Chard and beet are the same species wearing different jobs, and if you have read the [beet greens post](/blog/beet-greens-the-pretty-ones/) you already know most of the family story. Chard is the one bred for leaf and stem instead of root.

## How it tastes

Milder than it looks.

That surprises people. A stem that color suggests something is about to happen to your mouth, and then it does not. Chard microgreens sit between spinach and beet greens on flavor, softer than either, slightly earthy, faintly mineral, with no bitterness and no heat at all. The leaf is tender and the stem is crisp, and the crispness is most of the eating experience.

This makes it the variety to reach for when you want a plate to look like somebody thought about it but you do not want to change how the food tastes. [Mustard](/blog/mustard-brings-the-heat/) changes a dish. Chard decorates one and then quietly adds a mineral note underneath.

## Timing, and why it costs a little patience

Ten to fourteen days from seed to cut.

That is slow in microgreen terms. Arugula is out in about eight. What you are waiting the extra days for is color, because the stems deepen as they go, and a chard tray cut early is a paler and less interesting version of itself. Cut it too late and the first true leaves start coming, which is not a disaster but is not what you paid for either.

That slower clock is also why chard is never in a mixed tray. A [mix has to share one cut date](/blog/whats-in-the-salad-mix/), and nothing on a ten to fourteen day schedule can ride along with brassicas that finish in six or seven.

## Where to put it

Anywhere the color has to survive contact with the plate. Chard is best raw, scattered on at the end.

Over pasta, after it leaves the heat, [same rule as always](/blog/pasta-finishing-greens-at-the-last-second/). Across a grain bowl as the one bright thing in a beige lineup. On a homemade pizza once it is out of the oven. Wilted for a few seconds into risotto or polenta, which is the one hot application worth doing, because the stems hold their shape while the leaf goes soft. On a piece of grilled fish, where the pink against white is doing as much work as the flavor.

Nutritionally it carries the things chard is known for. It is high in vitamin K and vitamin A, brings magnesium and potassium, and the betalains behind the stem color are studied antioxidants.
