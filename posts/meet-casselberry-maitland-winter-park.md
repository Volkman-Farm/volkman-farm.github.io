The [Friday route](/blog/meet-winter-springs-and-oviedo/) exists because it is home. We would drive most of it anyway, and the parts we would not are ten minutes out of the way.

The north Orlando route is the opposite kind of thing. It exists because the car is already going. Monday and Wednesday evenings we are headed that direction for taekwondo and rock climbing, and we have written before that [the schedule is a family calendar](/blog/do-you-deliver-to-my-street/) rather than a sales territory. This is the route where that is most literally true. Nothing about it was drawn on a map. It was drawn on a fridge.

So it runs differently, and it is worth explaining how, because an evening route on a school night is a different animal from a sunrise one.

## Casselberry was a town built under slats

[Casselberry](/delivery/casselberry/) is the first stop. We cover the neighborhoods around Lake Howell, the Howell Branch Road corridor, the residential streets off SR 436, and the Lake Concord area.

It is also, and we did not know this until we went looking, a town that exists because of shade.

In the 1920s a grower named Gordon Barnett started a fernery in what was then Fern Park. The crop was asparagus plumosus, the feathery green that used to go into every florist's bouquet in the country, and the business was big. A Chicago couple, Hibbard and Mary Casselberry, came down to Winter Park on vacation in 1926, liked it enough to stay, and Hibbard went to work selling lots for Barnett before platting his own subdivision next door and calling it Winter Park Ferneries. In 1940 he talked the residents into incorporating their own town, and that is the Casselberry on the sign today.

Here is the part that stopped us. Plumosus cannot take direct Florida sun. So growing it meant building shade, physically, out of lumber. Slat sheds, acre after acre of them, some single ferneries running to dozens of acres of roof. An entire local economy under a structure whose only job was to subtract light.

We wrote [yesterday about treating shade as an asset](/blog/shade-is-an-asset-not-a-problem/) instead of a problem, and then found out that the first stop on our evening route settled that argument commercially about ninety years before we got here. There is something funny about it from where we sit. Our whole operation is a sealed room where we add light on purpose and pay to carry the heat that comes with it. A few towns down 17-92, an entire town got built by people nailing up boards to take light away. Same problem, opposite direction, and both answers were right for what was being grown.

## Maitland is organized around a lake

[Maitland](/delivery/maitland/) is next. We cover the Lake Lily neighborhood, the trail-connected streets, the residential corridors off Horatio Avenue and Maitland Avenue, and over toward the Art Center.

Towns tend to be organized around a road. Maitland is organized around water, and you feel it driving through: the whole middle of the place is a lake with a path around it and a park attached, and the streets arrange themselves in response. There is a farmers market at Lake Lily on Sunday mornings, which is the sort of thing a town only sustains if enough people there care where food comes from.

That market is also the reason a midweek stop makes sense here. Saturday and Sunday markets are wonderful and they are once a week. If you shop one, you know the shape of the problem: everything is perfect on Sunday, good on Tuesday, and by Thursday you are looking at a crisper drawer with regret in it. A Wednesday evening is the exact soft spot in that week. It is not a competing errand, it is the second half of the same one.

## Winter Park is the middle of the route and the middle of everything

[Winter Park](/delivery/winter-park/) is the heart of it. Park Avenue, the neighborhoods around Rollins and Hannibal Square, the Chain of Lakes streets, Audubon Park, and the area near Mead Botanical Garden.

It is a town of walkable blocks and old shade trees, and it has a genuine restaurant street, which is a rarer thing in Central Florida than people from elsewhere assume. If you cook professionally on or near Park Avenue, that is what [our wholesale page](/restaurants/) is for. Chefs buy differently than households do, in different volumes and on different notice, and it is worth having that conversation directly rather than through an order form.

For everybody else in town, the useful thing to know is that Winter Park sits in the middle of this route rather than at the end of it. On the Friday route somebody has to be last, and we have been [plain about what the last stop costs](/blog/meet-winter-springs-and-oviedo/). Here the geography is kinder. Casselberry to Maitland to Winter Park to College Park is a short, tight run down a corridor we would be driving regardless, and the spread between the first porch and the last one is small.

## Why this route is built for evenings

The Friday route is built around a sunrise cut. This one is not, and the difference is deliberate.

Greens for a Monday or Wednesday order come off the rack that afternoon, not the night before and not the morning before. They ride in the insulated cooler bag through the worst of the traffic, which is the whole reason the bag exists, and they reach a porch around dinner.

That timing does something for you in August that a midday drop cannot. We have written about [the clock that starts when a clamshell lands on your step](/blog/when-the-bag-lands-on-your-porch/), and the honest summary is that a porch in Central Florida is not a refrigerator and the hours it eats are the ones nobody counts. A box that arrives at noon sits through the hottest stretch of the day waiting for somebody to come home. A box that arrives at six lands as the day is letting go, into a house that is far more likely to have somebody in it. The odds of it getting carried inside quickly are simply better at that hour, and those odds are most of what the porch clock comes down to.

That is the design. Cut in the afternoon so the greens are hours old and not a day old, drive it when we are already driving, and put it down at the one hour when the porch is least likely to be working against us.

There is a fourth town on this run. College Park and downtown are next, and downtown is its own story, because that route exists for a completely different reason than this one does.

If you are in any of the three, [start an order](/order/) and we will put you on a Monday or a Wednesday.
