Slaw is the rare side dish that gets better after it sits. Salt and acid soften the cabbage, the dressing gets into it, and 30 minutes later it tastes like slaw instead of wet cabbage.

Most greens go the other way. Dressing on a tender leaf for half an hour turns it limp and dark.

So the question for a slaw is which greens can take the wait, and which ones go on at the table.

## Fold in

**[Kale](/greens/kale/).** We grow a curly type, and the curl holds dressing without collapsing under it. The sturdiest choice for a creamy slaw.

**[Broccoli](/greens/broccoli/).** Mild enough to disappear into the cabbage. If someone at the table won't eat anything green on purpose, this is the one to mix in.

**[Pea tendrils](/greens/pea/).** Thick stems, sweet, and they stay crisp longer than the leafy ones. Good in a vinegar slaw with carrot.

Fold these in right before the bowl goes out, after the cabbage has had its sit. They'll hold through a meal. They won't hold overnight.

## On top

**[Mustard](/greens/mustard/)** for a pulled pork slaw. Its heat [peaks at harvest](/blog/mustard-brings-the-heat/), and a small pile on top lets each person decide how much they get.

**[Cilantro](/greens/cilantro/)** for a lime slaw that's headed into a taco. Its fine stems go soft fast in dressing, so it goes on last, [same as taco night](/blog/taco-night-needs-a-crunch/).

## Before any of it

Wash the handful you're using right before it goes in, and dry it well. Water left on the leaves thins the dressing and speeds up the wilting. Keep the rest of the clamshell dry in the fridge.

If you're making slaw ahead for a weekend, keep the greens out of the container. Dress the cabbage the night before, add the greens when you serve.

Next slaw you make, split the bowl. Fold greens into half, leave the other half plain, and taste both again an hour later. You'll know which way your house likes it.
