None of the 12 plants on our food forest mounds is native to Florida.

We [listed them a week ago](/blog/the-food-forest-question/) and never once asked where they came from. So we went down the list:

- **Lemon.** Asia.
- **Mango.** South Asia. It's been grown in South Florida since the 1800s, which makes it feel local. It isn't.
- **Banana.** Southeast Asia.
- **Pineapple.** Tropical South America, by way of the Caribbean.
- **Sweet potato.** Tropical Americas, but not here.
- **Okra.** Africa.
- **Basil.** India.
- **Shampoo ginger.** Southeast Asia. For anyone who worries about it, it's not on Florida's invasive plant list.
- **Sunflower.** The big one everyone grows, *Helianthus annuus*, is North American but not a Florida native.
- **Butterfly pea.** The blue one people grow for tea is from Asia and Africa. Florida has its own, *Clitoria mariana*, and it's not the one on our mounds.
- **Blueberry.** Close. The Southern highbush varieties that grow here were bred at the University of Florida by crossing wild blueberries that are native to Florida. The parents are from here. The bushes we bought are hybrids built for fruit.
- **Seminole pumpkin.** Closest. UF/IFAS describes it as not technically native but with native tendencies. It's a squash that Seminole, Miccosukee, and Creek families have grown in Florida for centuries, and it behaves like it knows exactly where it is.

So that's 0 for 12 on a strict count, with 2 near misses.

## The field didn't do much better

The front 3/4 of an acre went under [a cover crop mix on August 1](/blog/the-summer-garden-that-feeds-the-fall-one/). 8 species in the bag. Sunn hemp is from India. Sorghum, iron clay peas, alyce clover, and browntop millet trace back to Africa and Asia. The corn is from Mexico. The sunflowers are the same non-Florida species as above.

Aeschynomene is the one. *Aeschynomene americana*, a low legume that grows wild in Florida and fixes nitrogen. 1 for 8.

## What the count tells you

It reads like a failure for about a minute. Then you notice almost nobody in Florida eats natives. Citrus came from Asia. Strawberries, tomatoes, and sweet corn are all bred for somewhere else and grown here in winter because it works. A Florida food system has always been an imported one, and there's no version of a food forest in Sanford that feeds a family on natives alone. The shed is no different. Every variety on [our rack](/greens/) started out somewhere else.

So the question isn't whether to swap the mangoes for something native. It's what natives would be doing if they were in there alongside the food.

The answer showed up in the front field. [The turkeys walking it](/blog/the-turkeys-were-here-first/) are the most native thing on this property, and they've been here longer than we have. The field wasn't built for them and they came anyway. Natives are for everything that isn't us. The insects that evolved with a specific plant. The pollinators that work a native flower and then cross over to your squash. The birds that eat the insects that would otherwise be eating your okra.

An edible feeds you. A native feeds the system that keeps the edible alive. Put them side by side and each one does a job the other one can't.

## The practical part

The easiest native swaps are the ones that do the same job as something you already planned to grow.

If you want a ground cover, Florida has a native sunflower, the beach sunflower, that sprawls low and flowers most of the year. If you want a flowering vine that fixes nitrogen, the native butterfly pea does it with a paler flower than the tea variety. If you want blueberries, you're already halfway there.

None of this means pulling anything out. It means that the next time a spot opens up and you were going to put something decorative in it, you have a list to pick from where every plant is also feeding somebody.

Take your own planting list, the real one, whatever's in the ground right now. Mark each line native or not. Then look at the blank spots on the property and ask what you'd put there if the only rule was that it had to be from here.
