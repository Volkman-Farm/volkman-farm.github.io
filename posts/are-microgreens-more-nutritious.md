Per gram, often yes. Per plate, it depends on how much you eat.

The study everyone ends up citing is from 2012. Researchers working with the USDA measured vitamins and carotenoids in 25 kinds of commercially grown microgreens and compared them with published values for mature leaves. Gram for gram, the microgreens generally came out higher. Which green led depended on the nutrient: red cabbage for vitamin C, [cilantro](/greens/cilantro/) for carotenoids, garnet amaranth for vitamin K, green daikon radish for vitamin E.

You'll see that study turned into "40 times more nutritious" in a lot of places. It doesn't say that. No single multiplier covers 25 plants and a handful of nutrients, and the range was wide.

## Where it gets complicated

**How it's grown matters.** A 2017 study on broccoli microgreens found that ones grown in compost came out higher in minerals than mature broccoli, and ones grown hydroponically came out a little lower. Same plant, different result.

**The comparisons aren't always side by side.** Both of those studies compared their microgreens against database values for the mature plant, not a mature plant grown next to them. A 2015 study did compare lettuce to lettuce directly and found the microgreens higher in several minerals. The research is growing, but it's still thin, and a 2018 review said as much.

**You eat less of them.** Nobody eats a microgreen salad the size of a head of romaine. A handful on eggs is a few grams, and a higher concentration in a small amount isn't automatically more than a lower one in a big amount.

## What we'd tell you

Microgreens carry a lot of nutrition for their size, and for some vitamins research suggests a lot more per gram than the full-grown leaf. They're not a replacement for eating vegetables. They're a way to add more to food you were already making.

Our own variety pages say what each green is high in, and [broccoli](/greens/broccoli/) has [its own longer write-up](/blog/broccoli-micros-do-the-heavy-lifting/).

If you want to see it for yourself, look up one green you eat often, like kale or broccoli, in the USDA's FoodData Central, and see what's in a 1 cup serving of the full-grown plant. Then picture what you'd have to put on a plate to match it.
