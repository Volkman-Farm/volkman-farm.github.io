A tomato belongs on a sandwich. A tray of tomato seedlings doesn't.

An edible crop can have parts we don't eat. The tomato is a fruit; a microgreen harvest takes the young stem and leaves. Those aren't interchangeable.

[Illinois Extension](https://extension.illinois.edu/news-releases/small-mighty-microgreens-are-easy-grow-indoors) specifically says not to grow tomatoes, eggplants, or peppers as microgreens because their stems and foliage contain toxins. A vegetable picture on a seed packet doesn't mean the seedling belongs in a salad.

For a first home tray, stick with crops grown for this use. [Kale](/greens/kale/), broccoli, arugula, mustard, and [pea tendrils](/greens/pea/) give you plenty to choose from. We grow all 5. Pick something you'd want on your plate, then look up the instructions for that exact crop.

The species is one check. The seed itself is another.

Buy seed sold for microgreen growing and read the treatment information. The [University of Saskatchewan's growing guide](https://gardening.usask.ca/gardening-advice/gardenline-nested-pages/food-plant-pages/indoor-veg/microgreens.php) says not to use fungicide-treated seed. It also says to read the package even when the seed is labelled organic. If the treatment information is missing, ask the supplier before sowing it for food.

Our [home-growing guide](/blog/can-i-grow-microgreens-at-home/) talks about garden packets costing too much for a dense tray. Cost comes after these checks. The packet needs to contain a suitable crop and suitable seed before its price matters.

Keep the packet with your growing notes, especially if you move the seed into a jar. A jar of small brown seeds loses its identity quickly without a label. Before filling a tray, you should be able to name what's going in it and point to the supplier's instructions for growing it as food.
