A correction first, because it is ours to make.

Our own kale page says to stir the greens into pasta in the last thirty seconds of cooking. That is wrong, or at least it is thirty seconds later than it should be. Thirty seconds in a boiling pot is a long time for a leaf that is nine days old. What comes out is dark, soft, and stringy, and it tastes like the idea of a green rather than the green itself.

The rule is off the heat, not in it.

## The actual sequence

Drain the pasta. Take the pot off the burner, or move the pasta into a warm bowl. Sauce it, toss it, get it to the point where you would be ready to eat. Then the greens go on, and you toss once more, and that is it.

The pasta is still hot enough to do everything you want done. Residual heat wilts a tender leaf in a couple of seconds, releases whatever aroma the variety has, and stops there. What you are avoiding is not heat, it is sustained heat. A hot bowl is a finish. A hot pan is a cook.

If you are plating individual bowls, even easier. Sauce in the pot, greens on top of each bowl at the table. They fall in as people eat and every forkful gets some.

## Which ones can take it

[Kale](/greens/kale/) and broccoli are the sturdy end. Their stems have enough substance to sit in a warm bowl and still read as a leaf a minute later, which makes them the right call when the greens are getting tossed through rather than piled on top.

Pea shoots wilt fast and that is not a failure, it is just what they do. Toss them in and they go soft and sweet and disappear into the dish. If you wanted the crunch, keep them for the top and add them last.

[Mustard](/greens/mustard/) is the one to be careful with. We wrote a couple of weeks ago that [its heat is volatile and leaves on its own](/blog/mustard-brings-the-heat/), and heat from a pan takes it out faster. Mustard goes on the plate, not through the pot, and it goes on in a small hand.

Cilantro is the same story for the same reason. Everything good about it is aromatic, and aromatics go up and out. Finish at the plate.

## How much

More than you think, but only once.

A generous handful per bowl looks like too much going on and turns out about right after it settles, because the volume collapses the moment the greens meet a warm noodle. What does not work is adding some, deciding it was not enough, and adding more to a bowl that is now three minutes cooler. The second handful never wilts the way the first one did and you end up with two different textures in one bowl.

Cacio e pepe with a pile of kale on top is a Tuesday dinner that takes eleven minutes and looks like somebody tried. [The whole list is here](/greens/) if you want to find your own combination.
