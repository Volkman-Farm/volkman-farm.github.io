We have a picture from the first week of July: the general manager at The Nazca Lines holding two of our clamshells, one stacked on the other, standing in the front of the house with the espresso machine behind him. Beet on top, kale underneath, our labels facing the camera. It is the kind of photo you only get from a place that is glad to see you.

The Nazca Lines is a Peruvian restaurant in Longwood, up on 17-92. We have been growing for that kitchen since before it was a kitchen.

## We knocked while it was still a construction site

We stopped in while they were still building the place out. No dining room, no service, no menu on the wall. Just a restaurant taking shape inside a building.

That is a strange moment to walk in holding a bag of microgreens, and it is also the best one. A kitchen still working out what its plates look like can build a green into them from the start. A kitchen that has been open two years has to take something off a plate to make room. Given the choice, we would rather be in the first draft than in the revision.

It is also the part of this business nobody tells you about. We are five people in Sanford with a shed. We do not have a broker, a sales rep, or a line in a distributor's catalog. What we have is the ability to drive somewhere, walk in, and hand a chef something to taste. That is the whole strategy, and it only works because we are small.

## What a standing order actually asks

Right now that kitchen takes [kale](/greens/kale/) and [cilantro](/greens/cilantro/) from us every week, with amaranth joining the standing order starting this week.

A wholesale account is not just a larger porch delivery. It is a number we commit to weeks before it is due. Seed goes in the tray on a schedule set by their service, not by our convenience, and the tray has to be ready the morning they need it rather than a day on either side of it. We wrote about what [harvest morning](/blog/harvest-morning-timeline/) looks like from our end. From a restaurant's end it is simpler and less forgiving: the greens are there, or a dish comes off the menu that night.

That pressure is good for us. A porch customer who gets their greens a day late is mildly annoyed. A chef who gets them a day late has already printed the menu. Growing for one demanding kitchen has made us better at growing for every porch on the route.

## The amaranth answer we owe you, and do not have yet

In [the beet post](/blog/beet-greens-the-pretty-ones/) we wrote about a chef on the wholesale side without naming him, and about an amaranth tray sitting on the shelf as a trial. The reasoning was that beet is the most striking thing we grow and also the crop we lose most often to mold, and that chef needed a reliable way to get magenta onto a plate. Amaranth was the hedge. We said we would come back and tell you honestly whether it worked, including if it did not.

That chef is this kitchen. Here is exactly where it stands, which is short of a verdict. The trial tray came up well enough that he has moved his standing order off beet and onto amaranth. That first amaranth delivery goes out this week. Nobody has plated it yet.

So this is not the post where we tell you it worked. It is the post where we tell you a chef looked at a tray and changed his order over it, which is a real vote and not a result. It does mean one line of the beet post is already out of date, and we would rather correct it in the open than quietly edit the old one and hope nobody noticed. We wrote that this chef takes beet every week. He did, and beet is the top clamshell in the photo we described up top. As of this week, he does not.

Whether the swap turns out to be a good idea is the part we still owe you. Ask us in a month whether it held its color through a Florida summer, whether it dodged the mold problem beet has, and whether he is still ordering it. That is a growing notes post, and it is coming.

## It was on the way the whole time

Longwood is not one of our [nine delivery neighborhoods](/delivery/), so people sometimes ask how the stop works. The honest answer is that it is barely a stop at all. The Nazca Lines sits a few minutes from our place, on the same road we already drive on Monday and Wednesday evenings to get the kids to taekwondo and rock climbing.

We have said before that [the routes are our week](/blog/do-you-deliver-to-my-street/) rather than a sales territory drawn on a map. This is that idea in its plainest form. We did not add a stop. We passed one. Somebody was building a restaurant on a road we already drove twice a week, we heard about it, and we knocked on the door before there was a door.

Restaurants do work differently from porches, and a wholesale stop gets built around the kitchen's prep schedule instead of ours. If you cook somewhere in greater Orlando and we do not already drive past you, [we will build the stop in](/restaurants/).

## Go eat there

We should disclose the obvious first: they buy greens from us every week, so we are not a neutral party here. Say it anyway. The food is genuinely excellent. Being glad to get in early had a lot more to do with the cooking than with the invoice.

[The Nazca Lines](https://nazcalinesrestaurant.com/) is on 17-92 in Longwood. Go see what they do with a plate.
