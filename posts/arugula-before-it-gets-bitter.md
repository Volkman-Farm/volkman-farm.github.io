Most people who say they do not like arugula have eaten it late.

That is not a knock on anybody's palate. It is what the plant does. Arugula is a cool-season green, and Central Florida gives it about ten minutes of cool season. Once the heat sets in, a mature plant reads the temperature, decides it is time to make seed, and sends up a flower stalk. Everything in the leaf changes at that point. The pepper turns harsh, a real bitterness shows up behind it, and the tender texture goes tough and papery. Plenty of people met arugula at exactly that stage, in an August salad, and reasonably concluded it was not for them.

Micro arugula never gets there. We cut it at about eight days.

## Same seed, different stopping point

Eight days in, the plant is two small lobed leaves on a thin stem and it has no idea that summer is coming. There is no bolting, no flower stalk, no stress response, because there has not been time for one. What you get is the pepper without the punishment. Sharp, bright, a little like fresh-cut grass underneath, and stronger than the grown-up version even though it is easier to eat. Concentrated is the right word for it. Refined is the other one.

That is the trick with a lot of what we grow, but arugula is where it is most obvious. Nothing about the plant got improved. We just stopped it early.

It also means arugula is one of the varieties that does not care what month it is. A bed outside in August is a losing argument. A tray on a shelf in [a shed we keep cool](/blog/keeping-a-grow-shed-cool/) is eight days, in August the same as in February.

## Put it on something rich

Arugula wants fat to push against. That is the whole guide.

The best version is pizza. A margherita out of the oven, hot, and a loose handful dropped on top so the leaves wilt slightly in the steam and nothing actually cooks. Steak, same idea, piled on while it rests. A burger instead of the lettuce, which is not a substitution so much as an upgrade. Shaved parmesan, lemon, olive oil, and it is a side dish with three ingredients. And in eggs, folded in at the last few seconds off the heat, the way we [do it with the milder varieties](/blog/microgreens-on-eggs-three-ways/), except here you will actually taste it, which is the point.

Nutritionally it does what the mustard family does. Arugula is high in vitamin K and folate, and it carries glucosinolates, the sulfur compounds that give the whole family its bite. [Broccoli is the one we wrote the long version about](/blog/broccoli-micros-do-the-heavy-lifting/); arugula is the one that makes you notice you are eating a brassica.

If your last arugula was bitter, it was probably just old. [Try it eight days in](/greens/arugula/).
