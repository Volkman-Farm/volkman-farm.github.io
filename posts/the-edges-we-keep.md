Our property line is about 1,230 feet long.

That number comes off the parcel map we had made back in June, the same one we used when we [mapped the yard before touching it](/blog/mapping-the-yard-before-touching-it/). I went back to the map for a permaculture principle. David Holmgren, who started permaculture with Bill Mollison in the 1970s, lists it as one of his 12: use edges and value the marginal.

The idea goes like this. Where 2 kinds of ground meet, woods and field, water and land, mown grass and tall grass, you get the plants and animals of both sides plus some that only live in between. More happens at an edge than in the middle of either thing. So a designer is supposed to want more edge, and curvy edges over straight ones, because a wavy line between 2 places is longer than a straight one.

So I measured ours.

## The front field

The front field is the biggest single thing on the property. Three-quarters of an acre, and its outline on the map runs about 830 feet.

That's a lot of edge, and we've never written a word about it. We've written about the field itself all year: the [sunn hemp](/blog/the-summer-garden-that-feeds-the-fall-one/), the summer mix, the mowing. We've written about the [turkeys](/blog/the-turkeys-were-here-first/) walking through it every few weeks. Never about where the field stops and the rest of the property starts, or what grows along that line.

## The run

The run is the opposite story.

Before the attack in August, the fixed run was a small rectangle. On the map its fence line is about 150 feet around. When we [rebuilt it](/blog/two-weeks-and-nothing-has-come-back/), the outer electric net alone came to 300 feet, and the inner net plus the divider is another 290. So the chickens went from about 150 feet of edge to nearly 600.

And we keep every foot of it short.

The grass along the netting gets mowed to about 3 inches, because anything green touching a hot wire bleeds the charge off. Between the outer net and the inner one there's a 5 foot corridor, a standoff, so anything that comes at the birds has to cross open ground and 2 lines of netting.

That's an edge designed to be dead. By the principle, it's a waste of the most productive line on the property. By the 3 birds we lost in August, it's the most important line on the property, and I'm not going to let it grow up to prove a point.

I don't think that breaks the principle. Holmgren's line has a second half: value the marginal. Sometimes what an edge is worth is what it keeps out.

## The low spot

There's one more edge, and it's the one I like best.

The lowest point on the property is a small depressional pocket the soil survey calls very poorly drained. Our oldest daughter's first garden bed sits right on it, and she's still keeping it going.

It's the one place where the wettest ground we have meets something we grow food in. I don't know yet what that edge does for her bed, or to it, and I'd like to find out before we put anything else near the low ground.

When we start [planting trees next year](/blog/what-were-designing-for-next-year/), the contours already tell us the trees go high and the water-tolerant plants go low. What they don't tell us is what to do with the band in between. That band is an edge too, and it's long, and it's on nobody's plan yet.

## Go measure yours

You don't need a parcel map. Pick the biggest single thing on your property, a lawn, a garden, a patch of woods, and walk its outline with your phone's step counter running. Then walk it again slowly and write down every plant you see in the first 3 feet on either side of the line.

Then decide which of your edges you're keeping short on purpose, and which ones you just haven't looked at yet.
