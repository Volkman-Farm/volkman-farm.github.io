At the end of the [cover crop check-in](/blog/cover-crop-check-in/) two weeks ago we said that what happens to all of it eventually was its own post and that it was coming in September.

The technique is called chop and drop. You cut the plant down. You leave it lying where it fell. There is no step three.

It feels wrong the first time. Every instinct a person has about yard work says the cut material is now trash and belongs in a pile somewhere else, ideally in bags at the curb. That instinct is expensive once you work out what is in the bag.

## What you are throwing away

A cover crop spends its whole life doing two things at once.

Above the ground it is building bulk out of sunlight and air. Below it, it is pushing roots down through the sand and holding on. When you cut the top off and cart it away, you keep the roots, which is real, but you take the bulk with you. Every bit of carbon and nitrogen that plant assembled goes into somebody else's truck.

Leave it where it falls and you get three things instead.

You get mulch, immediately. A layer of cut material lying on the surface shades the ground, and in Florida in September shade on bare dirt is worth more than almost anything else you could put there. It is the same argument we made about [wood chips](/blog/mulch-the-cheapest-tool-we-have/), except this mulch grew itself in place and cost nothing to move.

You get a slow feed. That material breaks down where it lies, and everything in it goes back into the top few inches of soil rather than into a bag. With legumes especially, cutting the top also causes some root dieback underneath, so you are feeding the soil from both directions at once.

You get to skip the hauling. This matters more than people admit. A technique you will keep doing beats a better technique you will do once and then quietly abandon in July.

## How ours went

Our sunn hemp went in last spring on the front three quarters of an acre. About two months after planting it was tall enough to be a problem, so we chopped it to roughly eighteen inches and left every bit of it on the ground.

Cut high, not to the dirt. Leave stubble, and let the material fall across it so it sits up off the surface a little instead of matting flat. Sunn hemp will often come back from a cut like that, which means you can get a second round of growth out of the same planting, and the stalks you dropped are already working while the plant regrows.

Then at the end of July we mowed the stand again, and two days later we broadcast a fifty pound bag of [Hancock's spring and summer mix](/blog/the-summer-garden-that-feeds-the-fall-one/) straight over the residue. One bag over the front three quarters of an acre, which is the amount we had, and close enough to what the ground needed.

That second mowing was seedbed prep, not chop and drop, but it shows the useful side effect. Dropped material is not a barrier to the next planting. Seed goes down through it, finds soil, and comes up, and the residue that was lying there stops the sun from cooking the seedlings while they establish. Three weeks later [the field was green](/blog/cover-crop-check-in/) and even, which is not the result you would expect from throwing seed across a mat of old stalks.

## The part we have not done yet

The stand out there now is eight species deep and still filling in, and it will get cut eventually. Sorghum and sunflowers and corn get tall and then they get woody, and there is a window where a plant has put on maximum bulk and has not yet gone to seed, which is where you want to be standing with the mower.

We have not picked that date, and we are not going to put one here to have something to put. We will walk it and decide when it looks ready, the same way we decided the first time. When it happens we will write down what we cut, what it looked like underneath, and whether the sand is still hidden.

## If you want to try it on a smaller scale

You do not need three quarters of an acre. Chop and drop works on a single bed and it works in a food forest, which is where we use it most.

Anything you grow that puts on a lot of leaf can be cut back and left underneath the plants you care about. Prune a limb, drop it under the tree. Cut back a sprawling herb, leave the cuttings on the bed. It looks untidy for about two weeks and then it disappears into the ground, and what is underneath is darker and softer than what was there before.

The rule that makes it work is boring: cut before seed, and leave it where it lands. Everything else is preference.

We still cannot get the shed to work this way. Spent tray material has to be carried out and it goes about a hundred and fifty feet to the chicken run, which is a wheelbarrow trip and not a philosophy. [The greens](/greens/) are the one thing on this property that will not compost itself in place.
