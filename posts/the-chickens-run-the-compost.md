We owned a compost tumbler once. Black barrel on a steel frame, the kind you crank a few turns when you walk past it. Nothing wrong with it. We cleaned it out and passed it along to family, and it is still in use over there.

What happened was that we built the mulch piles and the chicken run, and after that the tumbler stopped getting filled. Everything that used to go in it was going somewhere better. So it went to somebody who would use it.

That is the honest version of how composting works here. We did not design a system and then execute it. We ended up with one, and the tumbler was the piece that got left out.

Here is what actually happens now.

Harvest morning ends with a stack of spent trays. Once the greens are cut, what is left is a dense mat of roots holding a slab of growing media together, plus the stems and odd bits that never made it into a clamshell. It is heavy, it is wet, and there is a lot of it every week. That material is the biggest waste stream [the shed](/blog/the-shed-albert-built/) produces, by a wide margin.

It goes over the fence.

Not right away, and not conveniently. The run sits about 150 feet from the shed, which is far enough that nobody carries trays by hand twice. It is a wheelbarrow trip, every harvest, in a Florida July. That distance is the tax we pay for the loop, and it is worth naming, because plenty of homestead writing skips straight from "we feed the scraps to the chickens" to the nice part and leaves out the pushing.

The nice part is real though. The birds hear the trays coming before they see them. Chickens are not subtle. By the time the first mat lands in the run they are already lined up, and then they go at it like it owes them money. The tender leftover greens go first. Then the scratching starts, and the scratching is the real work. Within a day a mat that came out of the tray in one solid piece is confetti, spread across the ground, mixed into the litter, most of the way to being dirt.

Nothing in that run gets turned by us. That is the part the tumbler used to do and the part the flock does for free.

## The bin they cannot finish

Not everything out there is chicken food. Kitchen scraps come out too, and some of it the flock will not touch or should not have. That goes into a compost bin sitting inside the run.

We wrote about that bin back in [the permaculture post](/blog/permaculture-without-the-jargon/), and it is still the strangest good thing in this yard. Black soldier flies find it on their own. Their larvae do the composting, chewing through scraps far faster than a quiet pile ever would, and when they are ready to pupate they climb out of the bin by themselves. The chickens are standing right there waiting. Nobody harvests anything. The bugs walk to the birds.

That loop has earned its own post and it is getting one this week, so we will leave it at that.

## The piles are still ours

Outside the run, the labor comes back.

We keep mulch piles in the backyard, and those we turn ourselves. Not on a schedule. Not on a Saturday rhythm, not every two weeks, not when a book says to. We turn them when somebody walks past with a fork in their hand and enough daylight left to care. Sometimes that is twice in a month and sometimes it is not for a season. The piles are forgiving about it, which is most of why the method survives around here.

A pile that gets left alone long enough stops being mulch. It settles, it darkens, and after about a year it is a garden bed. Our oldest daughter's second bed started life as one of those piles, and she did not so much build it as inherit it. That is the whole trick with [sand like ours](/blog/what-florida-sand-taught-us/). You do not make soil over a weekend. You park something on a spot and come back next year.

## The hawks decided where the birds live

We should correct something we have implied before, because the picture has changed.

We have a chicken tractor: a floorless pen the birds live under that is meant to be dragged to fresh ground every few days. That was the original plan for this yard, the birds mowing and fertilizing their way across the property one patch at a time. It is a good idea and it is not what we do anymore.

The reason is hawks. Central Florida has plenty of them, they are patient, and a pen sitting out in the open on acreage is not a hard problem for a bird that hunts for a living.

We learned that the way most people learn it. We lost a bird, and our younger daughter was the one who found her. She cried for a full day. We went out for burgers that evening, which is not a homesteading technique, but it was the only thing anybody could think of that helped.

So we built the fixed run and put aviary netting over the top of it. That net is the whole reason the geography changed, because you cannot net a pen that moves. Cover the birds and the cover decides where they live.

The tractor did not go away. It just works inside the fixed run now: same pen, same birds, a much shorter drag, always under the netting. They still get put onto ground they have not wrecked yet. They only do it within a fence with a roof.

## What we give up

Honest accounting, because a closed loop is not a free lunch.

We do not get finished compost. There is no screened black crumbly heap to scoop into pots, which is exactly what most people picture when they hear the word. What we get instead is worked ground, fed birds, and a trash can that never sees a green scrap.

We also gave up tidiness. A tumbler is contained and a chicken run is a chicken run. And the flock is picky: woody stems and anything tough get walked past, and those end up on the mulch piles instead, on the slow timeline.

Still, the math is hard to argue with. Every tray we cut for a porch or a restaurant leaves something behind, and none of it leaves the property. The clamshells go out in the cooler bag, the roots go 150 feet the other direction, and the birds turn what we could not sell into ground we can plant. [Ten varieties](/greens/) head out the door most weeks and the leftovers never once make it to the curb.
