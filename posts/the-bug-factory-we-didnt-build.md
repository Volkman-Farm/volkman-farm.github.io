You can buy a machine for this. Purpose-built black soldier fly bins are a real product, and the good ones are genuinely clever: a sloped floor, a ramp climbing one wall, a bucket hanging off the end of the ramp to catch the larvae as they leave. Engineered, sold, shipped. People love them and the reviews are good.

We do not own one. We have a plastic compost bin sitting inside the chicken run, and the flies showed up on their own.

We mentioned that bin twice now, once [back in July](/blog/permaculture-without-the-jargon/) when we laid out the loops, and again [yesterday](/blog/the-chickens-run-the-compost/) when we admitted we gave the compost tumbler away. Both times we said the same thing in a sentence and moved on. It deserves more than a sentence, because it is the one part of this yard that works better than anything we designed.

## The fly nobody warns you about

Start with the insect, because most people hear "fly" and picture the wrong animal entirely.

A black soldier fly is not a housefly. It is bigger, darker, slower, and closer to a wasp in silhouette than to the thing circling your kitchen. The adult does not have working mouthparts. It cannot bite you and it does not eat. It emerges, mates, lays eggs near something rotting, and dies inside about a week. It has no reason to come indoors and no equipment to be a pest with. Research suggests that material already colonized by soldier fly larvae actually turns houseflies away, which is the opposite of what anyone expects from a bin full of maggots.

All of the eating happens in the larval stage, and the eating is the whole point. Soldier fly larvae are fast. They go through soft food waste at a rate a quiet pile cannot touch, and they generate their own heat doing it. Stick your hand near a working mass of them and it is warm. Melon rinds, fruit gone over, the vegetable ends that got past the chickens: all of it disappears into them.

## Then they leave

Here is the behavior that makes the whole thing run without us.

When a larva has eaten enough, it changes. It stops feeding, empties its gut, darkens up, and gets an urge to go somewhere else. Prepupal is the word for it. Wet, rotting material is a fine place to eat and a terrible place to spend a week turning into an adult, so it crawls out of the food and starts hunting for dry ground. It will climb to get there. That climbing instinct is the entire engineering premise of every commercial bin: they know the larvae are going to walk out, so they give them one ramp to walk up and put a bucket at the top.

Our bin does not have a ramp. Our bin has a chicken run around it.

The larvae come up the inside wall and over the rim on their own schedule, and the flock is standing right there, because chickens learn a food source faster than they learn anything else. Nobody sifts. Nobody scoops. Nobody puts on gloves. The protein walks itself out of the waste stream and into the birds, and the only labor in the whole arrangement is carrying a bucket of scraps out to the run.

That is what makes it worth a post. Every other loop on this place costs us something. Spent trays are a wheelbarrow trip in July heat. The mulch piles get turned by hand. This one asks nothing. The insect's own biology does the harvesting, and it does it in the one direction that happens to be useful to us.

## The honest part

We cannot take credit for any of it, and there is no control knob anywhere.

We did not seed the bin. We did not choose the timing. We have no idea how many larvae are in there on a given week, and no way to make more when we would like more. Florida gave us a warm climate and a rotting bucket and the flies did the rest. If they stopped finding it, we would have to go buy the machine like everybody else.

We also give up the finished compost, which we owned up to in [the compost post](/blog/the-chickens-run-the-compost/) and will keep saying. Larvae are not making you a bag of dark crumbly soil. They are converting scraps into insect and leaving behind a smaller amount of residue than you started with. What we get out of this is fed birds and an empty trash can, not a wheelbarrow of black gold.

Both of those are fine trades. The thing people miss about permaculture is that the best pieces of it are usually not built. They are noticed. Somebody sets down a bucket of scraps in a warm place, walks past it for a few weeks, and figures out that an animal nobody invited has already solved the problem for free.

The [greens](/greens/) are the part we actually work at. The bugs are on their own.
