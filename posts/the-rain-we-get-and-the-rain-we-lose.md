August storms here are not weather so much as an event. The sky goes the color of an old nickel around three, everything gets very still, and then the bottom falls out for forty minutes. Afterward the air smells like hot asphalt and wet oak and the whole yard is steaming.

We have been standing out in it on purpose this month.

Here is the arithmetic that started it. One inch of rain on one acre is a little over 27,000 gallons. We have 2.17 acres. So an inch of rain, which is an ordinary summer afternoon around here and not even a memorable one, drops something close to sixty thousand gallons on this property. Run the numbers yourself if you want, it is not complicated math, and that is sort of the point. Nobody has to be a hydrologist to find this number alarming.

Sixty thousand gallons. And we keep none of it.

Not some of it. None. There is no barrel, no cistern, no swale, no basin, nothing on this property that was built to hold water. Every drop that falls here either soaks into the sand where it lands or wanders off to a low spot and waits its turn to do the same thing. Meanwhile there is a hose out by the shed that we turn on when things get dry, and it is pulling water that somebody else pumped, treated, and piped to us.

Put those two sentences next to each other and the second one starts to look ridiculous.

## Where it actually goes

So we watched. Same as [the year we spent watching before we planted anything](/blog/mapping-the-yard-before-touching-it/), except this time with a narrower question: after the rain stops, where is the water?

A few spots pool. Not the whole yard and not evenly, just a handful of places where the ground is low enough that water arrives faster than the sand can take it. Then it sits, and it takes two or three days to clear, depending on how hard it came down. A short storm is gone by the next morning. A real one leaves standing water into the third day.

Two to three days is a genuinely interesting number, and it took us a while to work out why.

Everybody knows Florida sand drains fast. We have written about [what that sand does to a garden](/blog/what-florida-sand-taught-us/) and none of it is flattering. Water goes straight through it and takes everything you wanted to keep along with it. So how does anything stand around for three days on ground like that?

Because the sand is not the whole story. The soil survey calls most of this lot poorly drained fine sand, with one pocket that it calls very poorly drained, which is a soil scientist's way of saying it is a wet spot and always has been. And the water table under this part of the county sits high. We knew that already, because it is why [the shed pad got built up off the grade](/blog/the-shed-albert-built/) instead of poured flush. When the ground below is already full, the sand on top cannot drain into it. There is nowhere for the water to go. So it sits on the surface and waits for the table to fall.

Fast-draining soil over a full sponge. Both things are true and they are the reason this yard is dry and soggy in different places on the same afternoon.

## The part that stings

The frustrating thing is not the pooling. The pooling is honest. Those low spots are just telling us what they are, and the answer is not to fill them in. The soil survey and the contours already told us to put trees and mounds on the higher ground and put water-tolerant things in the lows, and a wet pocket that holds water for three days is not a problem to solve. It is a site condition, and eventually it is going to be a planting.

What stings is the other fifty-something thousand gallons. The rain that lands on high ground and is simply gone by Thursday, having done nothing for us on the way through except get the surface wet for an afternoon. That water fell for free, on our ground, and left because nothing was built to slow it down.

The mulch is doing a little. We laid three to four inches down [to build the yard toward worms and away from ants](/blog/building-for-worms/), and a mulch layer is a sponge whether or not you were thinking of it that way. Rain that hits mulch gets held in the mulch for a while instead of hitting bare sand and running. That is real, and it is also about the least deliberate water catchment a person could have. We got it as a side effect of doing something else.

Back in July we wrote that most of this yard is still in the watching stage and that the rain still leaves in ways we have not caught. That line was doing a lot of quiet work. We knew, and we had not done anything about it.

We have a list now. Where a swale would go if we cut one, which mounds would take a basin, what a gutter on the shed roof would actually be worth in a summer like this one. None of it is built. That is tomorrow's post, and writing it down is most of what we have accomplished so far.

For now the honest position is this: a good storm rolls through, we go stand in the yard afterward, and we watch sixty thousand gallons decide what to do without us. It is a humbling way to spend a Tuesday.

Meanwhile the shed does not care what the sky is doing, and the trays get [filtered water on a schedule](/blog/seed-to-tray-how-a-crop-starts/) either way. [Order for this week](/order/) and something green shows up regardless of the forecast.
