Something got into the run over the weekend. We lost three birds. Two more are hurt badly enough that they are not out there anymore.

We do not know what it was. We did not see it, and we are not going to name an animal we cannot identify. In Central Florida that list is long, and picking a favorite off it would be a guess wearing the clothes of a finding. What we have is what it left behind.

One side of the fence was pushed down.

Here is the part that has been hardest to sit with. This was a new flock, and they had only just started laying. A couple of weeks, that is all. You spend months on birds that give you nothing back but chores, and then one morning there are eggs in the box and it feels like the arrangement finally went both ways. We got about two weeks of that.

The fence is the part we keep coming back to, because of what we told you in July.

We wrote a section then called [the hawks decided where the birds live](/blog/the-chickens-run-the-compost/). The argument was that covering the birds is what fixes the geography. You put aviary netting over a fixed run, and the net is the reason the pen cannot move anymore. We meant it, and we still think it is right. But reading that back this week, it is a post about the sky. The threat had come from above, so we built upward, and the top of that box is the part we have thought about ever since.

Nothing came through the top. It came in at ground level, by leaning on a fence until the fence gave.

There is an electric fence on that run. We have never written about it here, which is its own small tell about how much of our attention it was getting. We think it was not doing its job. We think that because a fence with a live charge on it is a thing most animals argue with exactly once, and whatever this was clearly never had that argument. But we have not proven it, and we are trying to be careful about the difference between the story that fits and the thing we actually know.

## What we changed

Electric netting does not fail dramatically. It fails by degrees, and the usual way is grass. Green growth leaning on a hot wire bleeds the charge into the ground a little at a time, and the fence goes on looking exactly like a fence the whole way down to useless. So the first thing we did was mow the fence lines down to about three inches.

We cannot prove that is what happened. But it is the specific way this kind of fence fails, and the grass was there, and we were not cutting it.

Then we rebuilt bigger. The run is about twice the size it was, and it is two paddocks now instead of one, roughly fifteen hundred square feet each, with a divider down the middle. That means we can close one side and let it rest while the birds work the other. Ground that never gets a break stops being pasture and turns into bare dirt, which we have watched happen in slow motion. Rotation is the answer to that and we did not previously have anywhere to rotate to.

The change that is actually aimed at the predator is the one that is easiest to miss on a drawing. There are two lines of netting now, an outer and an inner, with a five foot corridor running between them the whole way around. The gap is the point. Something working on the outer line is still five feet away from anything worth reaching, and five feet is a long distance when you cannot reach through it.

The divider has a second job we have not needed yet. When you bring young birds into an established flock, you want them separated but in sight of each other for a while first, and a fence down the middle is the cheapest way to do that. We intend to add chicks at some point. Nothing is ordered and nothing is scheduled, so read that as a sentence about the future rather than an announcement.

The two that are hurt are in the brooder. That is not what a brooder is for, but it is the smallest, warmest, most controllable space we have, and it is the one place we can watch them without the rest of the flock working them over. We are checking on them often. We are not going to make a prediction here about how that goes, because we do not have one. Ask us in a couple of weeks and we will tell you either way.

There are five of us in this house and it was a bad weekend for every one of us. We are going to leave it at that.

If there is anything in this worth handing to somebody else with a run in a Florida backyard, it is not a fence design and it is not a product. It is just this. We armored the direction we had already been hurt from.

That is not a stupid way to think. It is an incomplete one, and it is an easy incompleteness to end up with, because the hawk arrived as a story with a lesson at the end of it, and the lesson was cover the top. So we covered the top. We did it properly. And then we stopped auditing, because the part of the problem we had already solved was the part we kept looking at.

We do not know whether what we built this week is enough either. That is the honest end of it. The last thing we built was also put up carefully, by people who had just learned something, and it worked exactly as designed against the one problem it was designed for.
