4 kinds of thing go from the shed into our trash can. Empty soil bags. Seed packets. Paper towels. Gloves.

Everything else the shed throws off stays on the property, and most of it ends up inside a chicken.

David Holmgren's sixth permaculture principle is *produce no waste*. He hangs 2 old proverbs on it: "waste not, want not," and "a stitch in time saves nine." Everybody quotes the first one. The second one is about maintenance, and Holmgren means it: timely maintenance prevents waste. That half gets less attention because nobody takes a picture of a repaired thing.

Our list reads differently with both of them in mind.

## Waste not

A cut tray leaves behind a slab of soil held together by roots, plus whatever stems and leaves didn't make it into a clamshell. That's the biggest thing the shed produces after the greens, by a lot. It goes in the wheelbarrow and rides about 150 feet out to the run.

The run is bigger than it was in July, 2 paddocks now instead of one, but the routine hasn't changed. The mat lands, the hens go through the green bits first, and then they scratch it apart until it's part of the ground. We [wrote up that loop](/blog/the-chickens-run-the-compost/) in July and it's still running the same way in late October.

Kitchen scraps the flock won't eat go in the bin inside the run. The black soldier flies [still find it on their own](/blog/the-bug-factory-we-didnt-build/), the larvae still climb out when they're done eating, and the hens are still standing there when they do.

The mulch piles outside the run get turned by hand, whenever somebody walks past with a fork.

And the A/C. The window unit that went in this month drains outside, onto the ground.

## A stitch in time

The second proverb is easier to miss.

The trays themselves are the best example on the place. A tray gets emptied to the chickens, hosed off, and sprayed with ZeroTol HC, and then it goes back on the rack for the next crop. We've always filed [tray sanitation](/blog/tray-sanitation-between-crops/) under food safety, and it is. It's also why we aren't buying trays. A tray that gets cleaned every time gets used again every time.

The paddocks are the other one. When they went in, the grass along the fence lines got mowed down to about 3 inches, because green growth touching a hot wire bleeds the charge off it. Grass grows back. Nobody notices that job until it hasn't been done.

## What's left

So, back to the can. Soil bags, seed packets, paper towels, gloves.

Those 4 come in from somewhere else and leave in the trash, and we don't have another place for them right now. They're small next to the trays. They still fill a can.

There's one more, and it doesn't happen here. Our greens leave in a clamshell, and [we don't take those back](/blog/after-the-clamshell/). Whatever happens to it happens at your house. Whatever was in it, [kale](/greens/kale/) or pea tendrils, our end of the loop stops at the porch.

This week, open your own trash can and write down every kind of thing in it. Next to each one, write where else it could go. Some lines will say "nowhere." Count those, and leave the rest of the list on the fridge.
