You build a good salad at seven in the morning. You open it at noon and find a wet gray pile with a puddle at the bottom. Nothing was wrong with the ingredients. The salad was assembled in a way that guaranteed that outcome five hours earlier, and it is worth understanding why, because the fix costs nothing.

## Wilt is a water problem, not a time problem

Five hours does not ruin a leaf. Dressing does.

A vinaigrette is salt and acid and oil. Salt on the outside of a leaf pulls water from the inside of it, straight through the cell wall, because that is what salt does to anything with water in it. The cells lose pressure, the leaf loses the stiffness that pressure was providing, and it goes limp. The acid softens the structure while that is happening. The oil coats the surface and seals the whole process in. That puddle at the bottom of your container is not dressing. Most of it came out of your salad.

So the leaf is not tired at noon. It has been actively dismantled since seven.

Once you see it that way the whole problem reorganizes. It is not about buying hardier lettuce or eating faster. It is about keeping the dressing off the greens until the moment you eat, and keeping the leaf cold and dry in the meantime. Do those two things and a five hour salad is nearly a fresh one.

## Some of our greens are built for the wait

Microgreens are not all the same in this regard, and the difference is structural. Some have a real stem holding them up and some are almost entirely leaf.

[Pea](/greens/pea/) is the sturdiest thing we grow. The tendrils and stems are thick and juicy and they have some actual snap to them, and a pea shoot sitting undressed in a cold container at noon is close to where it started. [Kohlrabi](/greens/kohlrabi/) is the other one people underestimate, with a crisp little stem that gives you crunch without much flavor commitment. [Kale](/greens/kale/) and the rest of the brassicas are reliable through a workday. Radish holds too, and it brings heat with it, which is a fine thing in a lunch that is otherwise a bowl of cold vegetables.

The tender end is a different plan, not a worse one. Beet is the most beautiful green on our shelf and it is also the first one to give up. Cilantro, mustard, and arugula are all better added at the last second than packed at dawn. We said something close to this in the [shelf life post](/blog/how-long-do-microgreens-last/), where the rule was to eat down from the tender end. In a packed lunch the same rule applies inside a single meal: pack the sturdy ones, carry the delicate ones separately if you want them at all.

None of this is a rule you have to follow. A cilantro micro that has softened a little still tastes exactly like cilantro. It just will not stand up on the fork.

## The order things go in the jar

A wide mouth jar or any straight sided container works. What matters is the stacking, and it is the same principle every time: put the wet things as far from the greens as you can, and let gravity do the rest.

Dressing goes in first, at the very bottom. Then the hard vegetables that do not care about being wet, which is cucumber, peppers, carrots, radish, red onion, anything with a rind or a snap. Beans and chickpeas belong in this layer too. Above that goes the grain or the pasta or the potatoes, and then whatever protein you are using. That middle block is doing real work, acting as a wall between the dressing at the bottom and the greens at the top.

Greens go in last, loose, filling the top of the jar. Lid on. Nothing gets stirred, shaken, or tossed until you are sitting down with it, at which point you either upend the whole jar into a bowl or shake it and eat straight out of the jar. Either is fine. The point is that the dressing and the leaf meet about ninety seconds before you do.

Two things to watch. Cooked ingredients have to be fully cool before they go in, because a warm grain layer steams the greens above it and steam wilts them as thoroughly as dressing does. And do not chop microgreens. They are already the size they are supposed to be, and cutting them opens up cell walls that were doing you a favor.

## Cold and dry, still

Our clamshell label says wash before using, and in a packed lunch that step moves to the morning. Wash the handful you are putting in the jar, then dry it properly, in a spinner or pressed between towels. Water left clinging to a leaf will wilt it on its own over five hours, with no dressing required. The rest of the clamshell stays closed and dry in the fridge.

Then keep the whole thing cold. An ice pack in the bag is doing more for that salad than the container is. A jar sitting on a desk in August is a slower version of the same failure.

That is the entire method. Dressing at the bottom, sturdy greens on top, dry leaves, cold bag, and nothing mixed until you eat. It is the same rule we gave for [taco night](/blog/taco-night-needs-a-crunch/), just stretched across five hours instead of five minutes. Greens go on last. Everything good about them is on the clock the moment something wet touches them.
