People ask about our light schedule expecting a number. Sixteen on and eight off, or twelve and twelve, something with a rhythm to it.

There is no schedule. The lights come on when a level has trays on it and they stay on until those trays are cut. No timer, no ramp, no night. We said as much in [the shed tour](/blog/the-shed-albert-built/) and it is still exactly true: two full-spectrum LED fixtures per level, twenty-four hours a day, for the two weeks a crop is alive.

The one number worth adding to that is the gap. The fixtures sit about a foot above the greens. The rack sets that distance and we do not adjust it, so it is a foot at seeding and a foot at harvest and a foot for every variety in between.

## Green is a thing light does, not a thing time does

Here is the part that makes constant light make sense, and it is the reason a tray coming out of [the blackout](/blog/the-blackout-phase/) looks the way it does.

A seedling in the dark cannot finish making chlorophyll. It gets all the way to the last precursor and stops, because the final step in that reaction is gated on light itself. The plant builds up the ingredient and waits. So a pale yellow tray is not a sick tray or a hungry one. It is a tray holding a loaded spring.

Put it under a lamp and the reaction runs. That is why the color comes on so fast that you can lose an afternoon watching it, and it is also why we do not see much point in giving a two-week crop a bedtime.

## The varieties do not arrive at the same speed

What does vary, and it is the only thing we really watch on this front, is how quickly each crop takes its color once it hits the light.

[Kale](/greens/kale/) is the fastest thing on our rack by a wide margin. Radish, [mustard](/greens/mustard/), and [arugula](/greens/arugula/) are right behind it, which tracks with them being quick crops generally. Put a tray of any of those four up after blackout and by the next time you walk in, the argument is over.

The colored crops are a different question, and it is worth saying that it is a different question rather than pretending we have timed them against the others. Beet, chard, and amaranth are not primarily trying to be green. They belong to a family that colors itself with betalains, a pigment group that is chemically unrelated to the reds and purples in most other plants, and the two have never been found together in the same tissue. When you watch a beet tray come up, the magenta in the stem and the green in the leaf are two separate processes running at once. Asking how fast it greened up only describes half of what happened.

## What we do not do

We do not run tender varieties on a shorter day. Everything on the rack gets the same light for the same reason, and the things we actually adjust from crop to crop are seeding density, blackout length, and water.

And we do pay for it. Constant light is [the largest heat source in the room](/blog/keeping-a-grow-shed-cool/) and the machines fighting it run on the same meter. That trade is settled. A tray in August looks like a tray in February, which is the entire reason the shed is sealed and lit in the first place.
