A food forest is a garden that's shaped like a woodland instead of like a row. Same idea as a forest edge, where things grow at different heights and different speeds and nobody tills anything.

The question people ask isn't what one is. It's what goes in first.

The answer most books give is the canopy, because the tree takes longest and everything else arranges itself around the shade it will eventually cast. That's correct on paper. It's also how people end up with 3 expensive trees standing in bare sand in July, cooking, while the owner waters them twice a day and wonders why this is supposed to be low maintenance.

## What goes in first

Ground cover. Before the trees, or at worst alongside them.

On [our sand](/blog/what-florida-sand-taught-us/) a bare surface is not neutral, it's actively hostile. It heats, it sheds a hard rain instead of drinking it, and it grows the weeds you didn't pick. Something low and sprawling over that dirt is doing work every single day from the week you plant it, which is not true of a mango for years.

Sweet potato is the obvious candidate here and it's what we have running. Seminole pumpkin does the same job with a bigger leaf. Neither of them politely stays where you put it, which is the point.

Second thing in: whatever fixes nitrogen and flowers for a long season. We have butterfly peas doing both.

Third: the trees. By then they're going into ground that's already covered, already shaded at the surface, and already feeding something.

## Layers, quickly

Canopy is the tall thing that eventually owns the vertical space. Understory is the smaller trees and big shrubs beneath it. Then herbaceous plants, then ground cover, then roots, and then vines running up through all of it.

You don't need all 6 and you certainly don't need them on day one. What you need is to stop planting in a single layer, which is what a vegetable row is and what most backyards are.

Our [mounds](/blog/how-you-know-a-pile-is-finished/) have a lemon, 2 mangoes, 2 blueberries, a banana, pineapple, sweet potato, Seminole pumpkin, okra, butterfly peas, sunflower, basil, and shampoo ginger in them. That's most of the layers by accident, because we planted what we wanted to eat rather than working off a diagram. [We've written before about reading that backwards](/blog/guilds-plants-that-work-in-teams/) and finding out what got covered.

## The Florida parts

Two things bend all of this away from the version you'll read in a book written somewhere colder.

Water decides placement before anything else. The rule we took off the [contour map](/blog/zones-for-2-17-acres/) is that mounds and trees go on the high ground and the water tolerant plants go in the lows. A design that's perfect on paper is still wrong in a spot that sits wet, and around here you find that out in one rainy season.

And the deep miner problem is real. Comfrey is the standard answer everywhere else and it's a poor fit in this heat. Moringa is what people use instead, it's happy in Central Florida, and we don't have one in the ground yet. That's the gap in ours and we know it.

The mounds themselves were mulch piles that sat a year. That's the cheapest version of this there is: pile organic matter somewhere, wait, plant into it. No till, no truckload of soil, no design certificate.

Go find the sunniest bare patch in your yard, the one that's been bare all summer. Don't buy a tree for it. Put something sprawling and edible on the dirt and leave it alone until spring, and see what the ground underneath looks like then.
