Yes for kids. They are vegetables, cut young.

That is the whole answer, and everything worth saying after it is about two things that have nothing to do with the plant: how big the pieces are, and how much of them ends up on the plate.

Size first. A pea tendril is long and springy and a small child will get most of a stem in at once and then have opinions about it. Snip it, or use the shorter tender varieties, or put the greens somewhere the kid is not going to encounter them as a mouthful of loose leaves. [We hid broccoli micros in smoothies](/blog/the-smoothie-you-wont-taste/) for exactly this reason, and [in a lunchbox](/blog/school-lunches-with-a-secret/) we press them into a wrap where they cannot shift around. Neither of those is a trick to get nutrition past a kid. They are just the versions that work at that age.

Volume second. Microgreens are a topping. A small handful over a bowl is the serving, not a plate of them as dinner. That is true for a grown adult too, so it is not a kid rule, it is just how the food works.

The one real safety line is on our label and it is the customer's step, not ours. **Wash before using.** Florida rules do not let us wash, rinse, or treat the greens after cutting, so nothing between the tray and your fridge has been through a sink. Wash the handful you are about to eat, right at the plate. That applies whether the plate belongs to a five year old or to you.

## Pets

Ask your vet, and that is not us dodging.

We grow ten leafy things and we are not a veterinary practice, so the honest position is that your vet knows your animal and we do not. What we can tell you plainly is that nothing on our list is in the onion and garlic family, which is the group that shows up in most of the real household warnings. A leaf that a dog gets off the counter is not that category of problem.

Volume is the question for a pet the same way it is for a kid. A stolen mouthful is one thing and a regular part of an animal's diet is a different conversation, and it is one to have with somebody holding your pet's chart. If your animal has any history of stones, mention that specifically. Some greens carry more oxalate than others and that is a real thing vets weigh.

Our own animals do eat this stuff, for whatever that is worth. The chickens get the [spent trays](/blog/the-chickens-run-the-compost/) after a cut, soil and roots and all, which is a very different thing from a clamshell of finished greens going in a dog bowl. One is livestock cleaning up a crop. The other is a decision about a pet's diet.
