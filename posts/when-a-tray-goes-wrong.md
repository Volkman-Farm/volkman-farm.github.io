We have written about diagnosis before. [The home growing post](/blog/can-i-grow-microgreens-at-home/) covers telling mold from root hairs and the three reasons a home tray goes bad, and we are not going to run all of that again.

This is the other half, the part that only matters if growing is your job. Something has gone wrong. Now what happens to the tray.

## The tray that comes up thin

This is the common one and it is undramatic. Germination is not even across the tray, so you end up with a stand that has gaps in it. Nothing is sick. There is just less of it than there should be.

Sometimes you can point at a reason. Seed that scattered unevenly, a corner that dried out, a spot the water did not reach the same way. Often you cannot, and the honest answer is that a tray holds more seeds than anybody is going to count and they are not all going to behave identically.

Here is what we do about it: nothing.

We do not restart a thin tray, because it is not sick and the two weeks are already spent. We do not top it up with a second sowing, because you would be harvesting two different ages out of one tray. And we do not stretch it by cutting lower into the stems to make up the weight.

What happens instead is that a thin tray just means less in the clamshell. Not less than you paid for. The clamshell gets filled the way it always does, and the shortfall comes out of how many clamshells that tray fills. The customer never sees a thin tray. The shed absorbs it as yield, which is exactly where a cosmetic problem should land.

That is the whole reason we are careful about promising volume before a crop is cut.

## The tray that molds

This one is not cosmetic and it does not get absorbed anywhere.

We have said in print that [beet is the crop we lose most often](/blog/beet-greens-the-pretty-ones/), and that is still true. Beet sits in the tray a full fourteen days with a seed husk that holds water like a sponge, and a Florida summer does the rest. That has not changed.

What we have not said before is that [pea](/greens/pea/) molds too. Not as often as beet and not as predictably, but it happens, and a big seed that soaked overnight sitting under a dense canopy is a similar setup for a similar problem.

A moldy tray is out. Not trimmed around, not cut on the clean side, not sold with a note. Out. Once you are making a judgment call about which half of a tray is fine, you have already lost the argument, and there is no version of that call we want to be making about food we are handing somebody. [Nothing we cut gets washed or treated afterward](/blog/tray-sanitation-between-crops/), which is the rule we grow under, and it means the tray itself has to be right or it does not go.

## Where a bad tray goes

Not to the chickens, which surprised us the first time we thought about it.

Every normal spent tray goes to [the flock](/blog/the-chickens-run-the-compost/) after a cut, soil and roots and leftovers together, and that loop is one of our favorite things about this place. But moldy material is not something we want the birds eating. So a moldy pea tray goes into [the black soldier fly bin](/blog/the-bug-factory-we-didnt-build/) instead, where the larvae work on it and eventually turn into chicken food themselves, one step removed.

It is a wheelbarrow trip either way. About a hundred and fifty feet from the shed door to the run, and which side of the run it lands on is the only thing that changes.

We like that the failure still has somewhere to go. A bad tray is a real cost, and it is a couple of weeks of light and water and six cups of soil that did not turn into anything sellable. It is not garbage, though. It goes back into the same loop everything else on this property goes into, just by a slower road.

If you want the varieties that almost never give us this trouble, the fast brassicas are the answer, and [they are all on the list](/greens/).
