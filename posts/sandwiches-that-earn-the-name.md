Think about what that leaf of iceberg is actually there for.

It is not flavor. Nobody has ever tasted a deli sandwich and thought about the lettuce. It is there for two jobs: something cold and crisp against all the soft salty stuff, and a barrier so the tomato does not turn the bread to paste. That is a real assignment. It is just that a flat pale leaf, sliced hours ago and sitting in a hotel pan, does neither one very well by the time it reaches you.

Microgreens do both, and they bring flavor as a third thing you were not getting at all.

We already wrote the packing side of this in [the school lunch post](/blog/school-lunches-with-a-secret/), including the fat as a wall trick and why a tight wrap beats a loose sandwich for anything that has to sit until noon. That still stands and this is not that post. This one is about what to put on a sandwich you are eating now.

## Match the green to the sandwich

**Turkey, ham, roast beef.** Anything cured or salty wants pepper against it. [Arugula](/greens/arugula/) is the obvious answer and it is obvious because it is right. If you want the heat turned up further, a small pinch of [mustard](/greens/mustard/) does what horseradish does on roast beef, and it costs you nothing in weight or crunch. Small pinch. It is a loud green and [it gets louder the fresher it is](/blog/mustard-brings-the-heat/).

**Tuna salad, chicken salad, egg salad.** These are soft all the way through and they need structure, not flavor. The salad mix is the right call here because the brassica stems have some snap in them, and a thick layer of it under the scoop gives you something to bite through that is not just bread.

**BLT.** Pea shoots, and it is not close. They are sweet, they are springy, and a bacon sandwich is the one place where a green that is genuinely crunchy has room to show off. [We called them the gateway green in July](/blog/pea-tendrils-the-gateway-green/) for reasons that apply exactly here.

**Banh mi, or anything with pickled vegetables and chili.** [Cilantro](/greens/cilantro/), obviously. You were going to chop a bunch and use a third of it. Now you are not.

**Grilled cheese, patty melt, anything hot and pressed.** The greens go in after the press, not before. Open it up, add them, close it, eat it. We wrote about [heat off rather than heat on](/blog/pasta-finishing-greens-at-the-last-second/) earlier this week and a panini press is the most extreme version of the same rule. Two minutes under a hot plate turns a tender leaf into a wet green line.

## Two mechanical things

**Use more than looks reasonable.** A sandwich compresses. A layer that looks absurd standing on the counter is a normal looking layer once the top slice goes on and you cut through it, and the cross section is half of why anybody wants this sandwich in the first place.

**Put them against the fat, not against the tomato.** Mayo, butter, cheese, avocado, hummus, whatever is on there. Greens sit on that layer and the wet ingredients go on the far side. This is the same principle as the wrap, and it is why a sandwich made in this order is still good twenty minutes later.

Ours are cut the day they go out, which is the entire difference between a green that does this job and a leaf that has been sitting in a pan since Tuesday. [See what is growing this week](/greens/).
