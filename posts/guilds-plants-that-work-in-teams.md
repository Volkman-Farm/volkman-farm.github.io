Lemon. 2 mangoes. 2 blueberries. A banana. Pineapple. Sweet potatoes, Seminole pumpkin, okra, butterfly peas, sunflower, basil, shampoo ginger.

That's what went into our food forest mounds. It's a grocery list, not a design.

Guild is one of those permaculture words that sounds like it needs a certificate. It doesn't. A guild is a group of plants put close together because between them they cover more jobs than any one of them covers alone. The version most people have heard is companion planting, basil next to tomatoes, marigolds at the end of the row. That's usually a two plant claim about pests or flavor, and a lot of it is folklore nobody's tested. A guild asks a bigger question. Not which two plants like each other, but what this patch of ground needs done, and which plants you'd actually want to eat that'll do it.

Roughly 7 jobs, and people order them differently.

Take the top. A tree or something tall that owns the vertical space and casts the shade.

Hold the middle. Shrubs and taller herbaceous plants filling the layer between canopy and dirt.

Cover the ground. Something low and sprawling that shades the surface so it stops cooking and quits growing weeds for you. Living mulch. In Florida it might be the most valuable seat at the table.

Fix nitrogen. A legume working with bacteria on its roots, pulling nitrogen out of the air and putting it somewhere the rest of them can reach it.

Mine deep. A taproot that goes well below everyone else and brings minerals up into leaves that eventually fall.

Feed the insects. Flowers, over a long enough season that something's always open.

Confuse the nose. Strong smelling herbs mixed in, on the theory that a pest hunting by scent has a harder time finding one plant in a crowd than one plant in a row.

## Reading our own mounds backwards

We didn't design ours as guilds. The [mounds are year old mulch piles](/blog/how-you-know-a-pile-is-finished/), and what went in them is what we wanted to eat and what'll take a Central Florida summer.

Hold that list against the 7 jobs anyway. You can see what got covered by accident.

Canopy is handled. The lemon and the mangoes will be the tall structure eventually, and the banana is already the biggest leaf out there. Ground cover is handled twice over, because sweet potato and Seminole pumpkin both run, and if you've grown either you know they don't politely stay put. Nitrogen has a candidate in the butterfly peas, which are a legume and which flower for months. Insects are covered by the sunflower and the peas and the okra between them. Aromatics by the basil and the shampoo ginger.

The deep miner is missing. Nothing out there is working the subsoil on purpose, and in sand that's the layer you most want something reaching into. Comfrey is the classic answer and it's a poor fit here. Moringa is the usual Florida substitute, happy in this heat, and we don't have one in the ground.

5.5 jobs out of 7, arrived at by planting what we like.

Two things push a Florida guild away from the versions you read about.

Ground cover stops being decoration. On [our sand](/blog/what-florida-sand-taught-us/), a bare surface in August heats, sheds a hard rain instead of taking it, and gives you nothing back. A sprawling edible shading that dirt is doing real work every day, which is why sweet potato earns its space in a year you don't dig much of it.

Water decides placement before anything else does. The rule we took off the [contour map](/blog/zones-for-2-17-acres/) is that trees and mounds go on the high ground and the water tolerant plants go in the lows. A guild that's perfect on paper is still wrong in a spot that stays wet (and around here you find that out in one rainy season).

So go look at something you've already planted. Not a new bed. One that's in the ground right now, that you walked past this morning. Run the 7 jobs against it and find the empty one.

Then put something you'd eat in the gap.
