We are not. And we do not call the farm organic, which is a separate thing worth saying out loud.

Here is what is true instead. The seed is certified organic, from [True Leaf Market](https://www.trueleafmarket.com/). The water comes off a whole house filter, the same one the rest of the house drinks from, and then runs through a Kangen unit that sets the pH. The trays get it at 6.0, which is what the plants want. Each one gets about six cups of Fox Farm Happy Frog. Nothing sprayed, nothing added, no fungicide, no fertilizer beyond what is already in the mix. We [walked through a seeding day](/blog/seed-to-tray-how-a-crop-starts/) if you want to see the whole list in order. That is the actual answer to what most people are asking when they ask about the seal.

## What the seal verifies

The USDA Organic seal is not a taste test and it is not an inspector deciding your greens look clean. It is a third party checking your paperwork against a plan you filed. You write an organic system plan, you keep records on every input and every lot, an inspector visits once a year, and someone who does not work for you signs off that the records match the practice. The value of it is that the verification is independent. Ours is not verified by anybody. It is just us telling you.

That distinction matters more than the word does. A farm can buy organic seed and still not be certified, which is us. A farm can be certified and be enormous and ship across the country, which is most of what carries the seal in a grocery store. The seal tells you a process was audited. It does not tell you the greens were cut this morning.

## What it costs

Worth being plain about the money, since that is the other half of the question.

Certification for a farm our size generally runs somewhere from several hundred to a few thousand dollars a year, depending on the certifier and your sales, since most of them work on a sliding scale. That is an application fee, an annual inspection, and a renewal every year after. There is federal help: the Organic Certification Cost Share Program reimburses up to 75 percent of what you pay, capped at $750 per scope. So the cash number is real but not ruinous.

The bigger cost is the recordkeeping, and honestly the bigger cost is that the seal buys us very little. It is built for distance. When produce travels through a packer and a distributor and a warehouse before it reaches a shelf, you have no grower to ask, so a certified label is doing the work of a conversation you cannot have. Our supply chain is a shed, a cooler bag, and a Telluride pulling into your driveway. You can just ask us.

There is also a rule on the books that lets very small operations use the word organic without certifying, as long as they meet the standards, under a gross sales threshold. We are not going to lean on it. Using the word because a threshold allows it, when nobody has checked our records, is the kind of thing that sounds fine until somebody asks a follow-up question. Easier to skip the word and hand over the input list.

## What to do with that

None of this is an argument against certification. If you are buying greens from a name you will never meet, the seal is genuinely useful and we would tell you to look for it. Certified growers went through something real to get it.

We are asking for a different kind of trust, the kind that only works at our size. Ask us what is in the tray. Ask where the seed came from. If an answer ever gets vague, that tells you something a label would not have. And if it helps, the label on your clamshell says to wash before using, same as anything else from a field, because that step belongs at your sink and at your plate.
