Change the seed, the soil, and the light on the same day, and a better tray leaves you with 3 possible explanations.

A worse tray does too.

We [weigh the seed for every tray](/blog/every-tray-gets-weighed/). The amount changes with the variety. The soil is about 6 cups of Fox Farm Happy Frog. Those are inputs you can put a number beside, which gives a home grower somewhere to start when a tray needs improving.

Suppose you're growing [kale](/greens/kale/) and want to compare 2 seeding rates. The question is narrow: does a little more seed give you enough extra harvest to be worth using?

Start with the supplier's guidance for your exact variety and tray size. If it gives a range, pick 2 amounts inside it. If it gives one rate, start there and get some trays grown before changing it. We don't publish our seed weights, and a number from somebody else's setup isn't a shortcut past learning yours.

For the comparison, use matching trays, seed from the same bag, and the same amount of the same soil. Sow them together. Keep the watering method, light schedule, and growing space as alike as you can. The seed weight is the thing you're changing.

Mark the trays before you water them. A and B will do. Write down which got which amount, the variety, and the sowing date. Memory gets less useful after both trays have turned green.

A few notes are enough:

- When the seedlings first come up, and whether the stand is even.
- Anything that happens to only one tray, such as a missed watering.
- The date each tray reaches the stage you'd normally cut it.
- The weight of each harvest, with the container's weight taken off the scale.

Keep the cut consistent. Extra soil or roots in one harvest won't tell you anything useful about its yield. Our [cutting post](/blog/where-the-knife-goes/) covers where the blade goes.

Then compare what you spent with what you got. More seed costs more even when the harvest weighs the same. An earlier cut might matter to you more than a small difference in weight. Decide what you're trying to improve before you pick a winner.

One pair of trays is a first look. Repeat the comparison before changing your routine, and swap their positions on the shelf for the next round. If the result reverses, keep that in the notes too. A result that won't repeat is useful to know about before you buy a bigger bag of seed.
