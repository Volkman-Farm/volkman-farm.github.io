Yes. We grow these for a living and we still say yes.

A tray of microgreens will go from a bag of seed to something you eat in about ten days, on a windowsill, in a house with no yard. That is a real thing to be able to do, and we are not going to talk anybody out of it. So here is the generous version of the answer: the setup we would actually tell a neighbor to buy, the method compressed, and the honest part at the end about where home growing usually stops.

## What you need, and what you can skip

Two shallow trays, the standard ten by twenty inch size. One with drain holes, one without. The solid one is a reservoir that the other sits inside, and that is how you will water once the crop is up.

A few cups of potting soil per tray. We use about six cups of Fox Farm Happy Frog, and we use it because it is a clean organic soil. That is the whole reason. The plant spends its entire short life in that inch of mix and you eat what comes out of it, so it is not the place to save three dollars. At home you are making the same decision at a smaller scale. Buy an organic mix, read the bag, and skip anything heavy or full of big bark chunks.

Seed is the one place we would tell you to spend. Buy seed sold for microgreens, by the pound or the half pound, from somebody who sells it that way. Ours comes from [True Leaf Market](https://www.trueleafmarket.com/). A garden center rack packet will germinate fine, but it is priced for four feet of row, and microgreens go down far thicker than a garden. You will run out halfway across your first tray and pay dearly for the privilege.

Light. A bright south window works. A twenty dollar shop light on a timer, hung a few inches above the tray, works better and more evenly. Our whole shed is a rack of shop lights over shelves, so we are in no position to tell you that you need something fancy.

And something flat and heavy. A brick is what we use. A second tray with a couple of cans set on it does the same job.

Water we will only touch on, because it deserves its own post. Ours is filtered and pH balanced to 6.0 for tender roots. For one tray at home, your tap is probably fine.

## The ten day version

This is the same method we run in the shed, at one tray instead of a rack. The long versions live in [seeding day](/blog/seed-to-tray-how-a-crop-starts/) and [the blackout stretch](/blog/the-blackout-phase/). Compressed:

Fill the holed tray about an inch deep and level the soil flat with your hand or the bottom of the other tray. Level matters more than it sounds. A high corner dries out, a low corner stays soupy, and the seed reads both as a reason to grow unevenly.

Then the seed, and this is the number worth getting right before you open the bag. Seeding density runs per variety, and it is not something to eyeball. Peas go down heavy. Small brassica seed goes down light. The gap between the two is bigger than you would guess. So look up the rate for the exact seed you bought, in grams or ounces for a ten by twenty tray. The page you ordered from usually publishes it, and if it does not, the variety name plus a search for its seeding rate will get you there in a minute. Weigh it on a kitchen scale instead of going by the handful.

What you are aiming for once it is spread is one even layer, seeds shoulder to shoulder without piling up. Too little and the tray comes in thin and grows lonely. Too much and the seedlings crowd and hold moisture against each other, which is where most first trays get into trouble. Dialing that number in for a new variety is most of the craft. Then water it in gently from the top so you do not push the seed into drifts and bare patches.

Stack the second tray on top, set the weight on it, and put the whole thing somewhere dark. Three days for most varieties. The dark tells the seed to send a root down before it reaches a leaf up, and the weight gives the stems something to argue with, which is what builds a thick even stand instead of a floppy one.

When you lift the lid and the surface has turned into a pale carpet of sprouts, the blackout is over. Yellow and almost white is exactly right at that stage. Under the light it greens up fast, often inside a day.

From then on, water from the bottom. Pour into the solid tray, let the soil drink what it wants, tip out whatever is still standing. Keeping the surface drier than the root zone is most of the difference between a clean tray and a fuzzy one.

Cut in another week or so with clean scissors, above the soil line, once the first leaves are open and the stand has some height on it. Cut what you are eating and leave the rest growing.

## Start with peas

If you are only buying one kind of seed, buy [peas](/greens/pea/). The seed is big enough that you can see what you are doing, it forgives a heavy hand and a light one, it soaks overnight and comes up with real force behind it, and the result is sweet enough that kids will eat it standing over the tray. It is what we would hand somebody who has never grown anything.

For a second tray, [kale](/greens/kale/). Fast, mild, forgiving, and it goes deep green under a lamp almost while you watch.

## What goes wrong

Almost always mold, and almost always for one of three reasons: too much seed, too much water sitting on the surface, or no air moving in the room. Central Florida in August is about the hardest place there is to start, because the air in your kitchen is already doing the two things you are trying to avoid.

One distinction before you throw out a perfectly good tray. Fine white fuzz that shows up overnight around the stems right at the soil line, almost like frost, is usually root hairs. That is normal and it passes. Mold is greyer and webbier, spreads as a patch instead of evenly across the tray, and smells like a basement. If you cannot tell, wait a day. Root hairs pull back as the tray drinks. Mold does not.

The other two failures are easier to read. A pale stretched stand that leans over means the light is too weak or too far away. Patchy germination usually traces back to uneven soil or an uneven scatter, which means it happened on day one and you found out on day four.

And the obvious thing, said plainly: we lose trays too. Beet is our problem child and always has been, enough that [a kitchen we grow for moved off it](/blog/did-the-amaranth-work/). A tray going wrong does not mean you are bad at this.

## Where it stops being fun

Growing one tray is a project, and a satisfying one. Having microgreens on the counter every week, so there is always something ready to cut, is a different animal. That is three or four trays running at staggered stages, a place to keep them, a light on a timer, a watering rhythm you do not get to skip, and a soil bag and a seed order to stay ahead of. None of it is hard. It is just relentless, and a Florida August is relentless back at you.

Most home setups do not die on the first tray. They die on the fourth.

So grow the tray. If it works you will have eaten something you made in ten days, and if it fails you will know precisely what you are paying us for. Either way you come out of it knowing more about what is in the clamshell. And if you would rather have it cut in the morning and left on the porch, [that is the whole reason we are here](/order/).
