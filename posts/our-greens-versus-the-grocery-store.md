Somebody asks this nearly every time we explain what we do: how are your greens any different from the ones at the store? It is a fair question, and the honest answer is not about us being clever. It is about time and distance, and those two do most of the work.

The store green has a long trip behind it. Most produce in a grocery cooler was cut days ago, sometimes a week or more, often a few states away, and it spent that whole stretch getting boxed, trucked, held cold, trucked again, and stocked. None of that is a scandal. It is just how you get lettuce to every store in the country all year round. But every one of those steps is time off the clock, and a cut green only has so much clock in it. Ours is cut the morning it comes to you, from a shed about three miles up the road. We wrote the [hour-by-hour version of that morning](/blog/harvest-morning-timeline/) if you want to see it. The short version is that "fresh" here is counted in hours, not in how recently the truck backed up to the loading dock.

## Cut, then bagged, then waiting

There is a second difference people do not think about, and it lives in the handling.

A lot of grocery salad greens are washed, sometimes more than once, and sealed in a bag or a tub before they ever reach the shelf. That is what makes them grab-and-go, and it is also what starts the clock running faster. A cut leaf, a little water, and a sealed bag is a slow decline no matter how clean the room was. [We do not touch our greens after we cut them](/blog/tray-sanitation-between-crops/). They go from the tray straight into the clamshell. Our label says to wash before using, so that step is yours, at your own sink, on the portion you are about to eat. Keeping them dry until then is a good part of why they [hold up as long as they do](/blog/how-long-do-microgreens-last/) in your fridge.

## The tub in the produce aisle

Some stores do carry actual microgreens now, in little plastic tubs over by the herbs, and we are glad the category is growing. But the same math applies, only more so. A delicate green is even less forgiving of a long trip than a sturdy one. By the time a shipped tub of micros is sitting on a shelf with a date printed a week out, it has already spent the best part of its life in a truck. It is not that they are bad. It is that the good part happened before you got there.

## What you can actually know

The last difference is the one we like most, and it is not really about the greens at all. It is about what you can find out. With a store green you usually cannot tell when it was cut or where. The [organic seal on it](/blog/why-were-not-usda-organic-certified/) tells you a process got audited, not that the leaf is fresh. With ours you know exactly: cut this morning, by the family that grew it, a few minutes from your porch.

Younger leaves also tend to carry higher concentrations of some nutrients, and [we wrote the careful version of that with broccoli](/blog/broccoli-micros-do-the-heavy-lifting/). But we would not lead with it. Lead with the plain part instead. A green cut this morning tastes like more, and lasts longer in the drawer, than one that has been on the road since last week.
