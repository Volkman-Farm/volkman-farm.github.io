Cilantro is the slowest thing we grow, and it is not close.

Eighteen to twenty-one days from seed to cut. Set that against [broccoli at eight to ten](/blog/broccoli-micros-do-the-heavy-lifting/) and you can see what it costs. One rack space, one dose of light, three weeks of tending, and at the end of it you have a single tray instead of the two and a half that same shelf would have turned out in something fast.

The seed is fussy before it even goes down. Coriander seed gets cracked before sowing rather than dropped in whole, and then the tray spends its first stretch doing very little that looks like progress. In a Florida August, three weeks damp is also three weeks of chances for something to go wrong, which is the same arithmetic that makes [beet the crop we lose most often](/blog/beet-greens-the-pretty-ones/).

We give it the space anyway, because nothing substitutes for it. Nobody who wants cilantro will take something else instead. Ask anybody making salsa on a Tuesday. Slow is what it costs, so we build the schedule around it rather than arguing with it.

## About the flavor, briefly

We are not going to tell you these are mild. They are not. Cut at three weeks this is cilantro at full volume, bright and citrusy, on a thinner and more delicate stem than a grocery store bunch. Nothing about the flavor got turned down. Albert cuts a tray and then smells like cilantro for the rest of the day, which he has decided he does not mind.

And if the herb tastes like soap to you, a smaller leaf will not fix that. Research points to ordinary variation in the smell receptor genes that read the aldehydes in cilantro, the same family of compounds that turns up in soap. That is a receptor, not fussiness, and no amount of good farming argues with it. We made the longer version of this case [on taco night](/blog/taco-night-needs-a-crunch/), including the one conversion we know best, and it is better linked than run twice.

## Getting it home

This is one of the tender ones. It gets cut last on [harvest morning](/blog/harvest-morning-timeline/) so it spends the least time off the tray, and it is happiest in the [first four or five days](/blog/how-long-do-microgreens-last/) rather than the back end of the week. Store it dry and cold. Our label says wash before using, so wash the handful right before it goes in the bowl.

Guacamole and fresh salsa are the obvious uses. A green sauce is the better one: chimichurri, salsa verde, zhoug, anything where cilantro is the body of the sauce instead of a sprig sitting on top of it. Float a pile on pho or tortilla soup right as the bowl lands.

Nutritionally it is high in vitamin K and vitamin A and carries antioxidants. You will also run into headlines about cilantro binding heavy metals. That research exists, it is early, and it is nowhere near the claim the internet has built on top of it.

Three weeks of shelf space for one tray, and [it sells at $3.50 an ounce](/greens/cilantro/) like everything else on the list. We have never charged more for the slow ones.
